# Human Communication Differences: Research Pass 3
## What Actually Works: Evidence-Based Communication Interventions

**Scope:** Neurotypical adults in workplace and team settings. Randomized controlled studies, meta-analyses, and field experiments prioritized over self-report surveys. Vendor-funded and low-quality sources flagged explicitly.

**Workflow stats:** 113 agents, 30 sources fetched, 82 claims extracted, 25 verified, 8 confirmed, 17 killed, 6 after synthesis.

**Research threads:** Perspective-taking interventions, structured communication protocols, feedback framing/timing/specificity, shared mental models, cognitive load and audience design.

---

## Summary

Evidence supports several discrete communication interventions, but effect sizes and conditions matter. Structured debriefs produce reliable 20-25% performance improvements (d=0.67), and shared mental models explain meaningful variance in team outcomes beyond behavioral factors alone. Active training methods (simulation, team reviews) produce large effects on teamwork behaviors while passive lecture-based training does not. Perspective-taking is genuinely double-edged: it can improve cooperation but demonstrably backfires in unequal-contribution settings, making unconditional deployment risky. Feedback timing and specificity interact -- the worst combination is infrequent plus global, not simply "less feedback." Structuring messages for specific audiences is cognitively demanding, with working memory as a limiting factor.

## Confirmed Findings

### 1. Structured Debriefs

**Finding:** Structured debriefs improve individual and team performance by approximately 20-25% over a control group (d = 0.67), based on a meta-analysis of 46 samples with 2,136 participants. Two subsequent meta-analyses replicate the finding at d = 0.79 and d = 0.92.
**Confidence:** High | **Vote:** 3-0

Tannenbaum and Cerasoli (2013, *Human Factors*) meta-analyzed 46 samples (N=2,136) and reported d=0.67 for properly conducted debriefs. Two subsequent meta-analyses replicated at d=0.79 (61 studies, N=3,499) and d=0.92 (83 studies).

Critical qualifier: the "properly conducted" qualifier is load-bearing. The original source explicitly states after-action reviews are not summarily effective and that context moderates outcomes substantially. A debrief done poorly or in the wrong context does not produce these gains. What makes a debrief "properly conducted" is itself an open question -- the meta-analysis does not decompose which design elements drive the effect.

**What was refuted:** The claim that debrief gains hold "consistently across team and individual formats, simulated and real-world settings, and medical and non-medical domains" was killed 0-3. The gains are real but context-dependent, not universal.

*Source: [Tannenbaum & Cerasoli 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23516804/)*

---

### 2. Active vs. Passive Training Methods

**Finding:** Active training methods (simulation, d=0.78; team reviews, d=0.64) produce significant improvements in teamwork behaviors, while passive didactic instruction (d=0.19, non-significant) does not. This finding comes from a meta-analysis of 72 controlled interventions (N=8,439).
**Confidence:** High | **Vote:** 2-1

McEwan et al. (2017, *PLOS ONE*) used random-effects meta-analysis of 72 controlled interventions (N=8,439) and tested publication bias via Egger's test and fail-safe N. The didactic subgroup had fewer studies than the simulation subgroup, reducing power, so d=0.19 is non-significant rather than proven zero. A 2022 simulation-focused meta-analysis (Hedge's g=0.41) corroborates active-method superiority.

**Practical implication:** Communication workshops built on slides and lecture do not produce measurable behavior change. Structured practice, simulation, team reviews, and after-action debriefs do. If you can't make it active, it probably won't work.

*Source: [McEwan et al. 2017](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0169604)*

### 3. Shared Mental Models

**Finding:** Team cognition (shared mental models and related constructs) has a moderate positive meta-analytic relationship with team performance (rho approximately 0.35, explaining ~14% of variance) across 231 correlations from 65 independent studies, and explains significant incremental variance in team performance beyond behavioral and motivational factors alone (~6.8% incremental R-squared).
**Confidence:** High | **Vote:** 3-0

DeChurch and Mesmer-Magnus (2010, *Journal of Applied Psychology*) synthesized 231 correlations from 65 independent studies. The 2021 Niler et al. continuation study replicated the core result with a nearly doubled database.

Important measurement caveat: questionnaire-based operationalizations of shared mental models yielded near-zero or slightly negative correlations with process variables (rho = -0.05) in a companion measurement meta-analysis. The rho = 0.35 result is not uniformly reproducible across all operationalizations -- it depends on how "shared mental model" is measured. The directional finding is credible; the magnitude is measurement-dependent.

**What this means practically:** Building shared context (explicit norms, common vocabulary, role clarity) is not just "nice to have" culture work. It contributes to team performance beyond what behavioral and motivational factors explain. But self-report surveys about whether people "feel aligned" are a poor proxy -- the effect doesn't reliably show up in questionnaire-based measures.

**Open question:** What specific interventions (team charters, explicit norm-setting, glossaries, structured onboarding) have been tested in controlled workplace studies and shown to produce measurable SMM improvements that then propagate to performance? This remains unresolved.

*Sources: [DeChurch & Mesmer-Magnus 2010](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-cognitive-underpinnings-of-effective-teamwork:-DeChurch-Mesmer-Magnus/f5bbfdf33d6fbbbd9f6614894c5151dcaa42623e)*

### 4. Perspective-Taking: Double-Edged

**Finding:** Perspective-taking in workplace settings is double-edged. It can improve cooperative interactions and team performance quality, but can also produce negative outcomes including less favorable evaluations, negative emotions, and exclusion. Specifically, when high contributors are instructed to consider others' contributions, they end up less satisfied, less happy with labor division, and less willing to work with the group compared to low contributors.
**Confidence:** High | **Vote:** 3-0

Halfmann and Thürmer (2025, *Behavioral Sciences*, PRISMA scoping review of 10 studies) with the backfire effects tracing to Caruso, Epley, and Bazerman (2006, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, four experiments). A synthesis of 118 studies over 30 years found perspective-taking can push toward win-lose outcomes and highlight differences rather than reduce them.

The negative effect is moderated by attribution -- whether poor performance is attributed to effort choice vs. ability -- and perceived pro-group intent. It is not mechanically guaranteed, but it is not rare or edge-case either.

**What was refuted:** The claim that perspective-taking outcomes are determined by "the inferences reached about intent" rather than "intensity of effort" was killed 0-3. The moderating mechanism is real but more complex than that formulation captures.

**Practical implication:** Do not deploy perspective-taking exercises unconditionally in groups with unequal contribution, or in situations where attribution of poor performance is live. The intervention needs to be matched to the context, and its backfire conditions need to be understood before using it.

*Sources: [Halfmann & Thürmer 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12108877/)*

### 5. Feedback: The Infrequent/Global Interaction

**Finding:** The worst feedback combination for performance is infrequent delivery paired with global (non-specific) content. The infrequent/global group showed a mean error rate of 9.94% versus 5.67-5.75% for all other conditions in a controlled experiment with random assignment.
**Confidence:** Medium | **Vote:** 3-0

Park et al. (2019, *Journal of Organizational Behavior Management*, Vol. 39) used random assignment of approximately 80 participants across four conditions in a computerized work simulation. Mean error rates: frequent/global 5.74%, frequent/specific 5.67%, infrequent/global 9.94%, infrequent/specific 5.75%.

Limitations: single lab study, computerized task, ~20 participants per condition. Real-world generalizability not established. The inverted-U literature complicates "more frequency is always better" at the high-frequency extreme, but does not dispute the infrequent+global interaction specifically.

**What was refuted (and matters):** Multiple seemingly solid feedback claims did not survive verification:
- "High-information feedback (task + process + self-regulation) produces d=0.99 vs. corrective d=0.46" -- killed 0-3
- "Feedback at the 'self' level (praise) is least effective; task- and process-level feedback is better" -- killed 0-3
- "Overall effect of feedback on outcomes is medium (d=0.48) with high heterogeneity" -- killed 0-3
- "Specific feedback is more effective than global only when infrequent" -- killed 0-3
- "Feedback frequency has a significant main effect on performance" -- killed 0-3

The pattern of refutals suggests the feedback literature is more heterogeneous and contested than practitioner summaries imply. The one thing that did survive: the combination of infrequent + global is clearly bad. Beyond that, the evidence for specific prescriptions is weak.

*Source: [Park et al. 2019](http://t.johnsonresearchlab.com/publications/2019ParkJohnsonMoonLee_The%20Interaction%20Effects%20of%20Frequency%20and%20Specificity.pdf)*

### 6. Cognitive Load and Audience Design

**Finding:** Structuring messages for a specific audience (audience design) is a cognitively resource-demanding task that actively engages working memory. Working memory capacity moderates communication quality under cognitive load conditions.
**Confidence:** Medium | **Vote:** 2-1 (one verifier flagged limited direct source verification due to PDF access)

Navarro, Macnamara, Glucksberg, and Conway (2020, *Discourse Processes*, Vol. 57). Corroborating evidence: working memory and perspective-taking research found verbal working memory affects the calculation phase of perspective-taking in adults; amnesia research found deliberate audience adaptation was impaired in hippocampal amnesic patients. Counterevidence: Horton and Gerrig (2005, *Cognition*) showed some aspects of audience design can operate through implicit memory mechanisms in routine conversation -- this complicates but does not refute the claim for deliberate message structuring.

**Practical implication:** Tailoring communication to a specific audience is not effortless. Under high cognitive load (high-stakes meetings, complex topics, time pressure), message quality degrades. The implication is to do the audience-design work in advance (prepared materials, structured formats) rather than expecting it to happen in real-time under load.

**Open question:** What practical communication design features -- chunking, redundancy, visual scaffolding -- have been validated in workplace-specific experiments rather than lab referential communication tasks?

## What Did Not Survive

Many commonly cited figures in the feedback and communication literature did not survive adversarial verification. Notable kills:

| Claim | Vote | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| Debrief gains hold across all contexts and formats | 0-3 | Overclaiming -- context moderates substantially |
| Proactive-support verbal patterns predict meeting satisfaction | 0-3 | Stats not verifiable to source |
| Meeting satisfaction predicts team performance (b=0.57) | 0-3 | Stats not verifiable to source |
| High-information feedback produces d=0.99 | 0-3 | Stats not verifiable at those figures |
| Feedback at "self" level (praise) is least effective | 0-3 | Not verifiable from cited source |
| Overall feedback effect is d=0.48 with I²=83% | 0-3 | Not verifiable from cited source |
| Specific > global feedback only when infrequent | 0-3 | Did not hold in full data |
| Frequency has significant main effect on performance | 0-3 | Not supported as standalone claim |
| Communication quality correlates at rho=0.36 vs. frequency rho=0.19 | 0-3 | Stats not verifiable to source |
| Information elaboration correlates rho=0.52 with performance | 0-3 | Stats not verifiable to source |
| Multi-dimensional training outperforms single-dimension (d=0.65-0.98 vs. d=0.05) | 0-3 | Not supported |

The volume of killed claims from well-regarded sources (PMC publications, UCSD-hosted research) is notable. It does not mean those papers are fraudulent -- it means the specific figures and framings circulating in practitioner summaries are often overclaims relative to what the primary research actually demonstrates.

## Open Questions After All Three Passes

1. **What makes a debrief "properly conducted"?** The meta-analytic gain is real and replicated; the design features that drive it vs. the inert ones are not decomposed.

2. **Does perspective-taking backfire in real workplaces the same way it does in lab studies?** The moderating variables (attribution, perceived pro-group intent) are identified but have a narrow empirical base (10 studies in the scoping review).

3. **What specific shared-context-building interventions** (team charters, glossaries, explicit norms) have been tested in controlled workplace studies and shown to produce measurable shared mental model improvements?

4. **What practical information-design features** (chunking, visual scaffolding, redundancy) have been validated in workplace settings rather than lab referential communication tasks?

5. **DISC and Insights Discovery** -- the commercial framework question is still only answered for MBTI. No peer-reviewed evidence surfaced for DISC or Insights specifically.

## Cross-Pass Synthesis

Across all three research passes, the picture that emerges:

**What does not work (or is not validated):**
- Typology tools (MBTI, likely DISC/Insights) -- no empirical basis for discrete types predicting communication or performance
- Learning style matching (VAK) -- debunked
- Hofstede VSM 2013 measurement instrument -- broken psychometrics
- Passive lecture-based communication training -- non-significant effects
- Perspective-taking deployed unconditionally in groups with unequal contribution -- backfires
- Most specific feedback prescriptions beyond "avoid infrequent + global"

**What does work (with constraints):**
- Structured debriefs, properly conducted -- d=0.67 to d=0.92, well-replicated
- Active practice (simulation, team reviews) over passive instruction -- d=0.64 to d=0.78
- Building shared mental models / shared context -- rho ~0.35 with performance, measurement-dependent
- Avoiding the infrequent/global feedback combination -- clear lab evidence, limited field evidence
- Designing for audience in advance rather than under load -- working-memory-limited, best done in preparation

**What the evidence can't yet answer:**
- The specific debrief design features that drive the gains
- Whether perspective-taking interventions work reliably outside lab conditions
- What practical communication design choices improve comprehension across varied audiences in field settings
